The Loyalty Tax: What Never Comparing Plans Costs You

Staying put feels safe. Auto-renewing without ever comparing is its own quiet decision, and it has a cost.

September 15·4 min read
By Michael von Heesen — Licensed Insurance Agent, Brightwater Insurance Group

If you do nothing during Open Enrollment, your current Medicare plan simply continues. No form to sign, no call to make — it just renews on its own. That convenience is real, and it's also worth looking at honestly.

What "loyalty" actually means here

With most services, staying loyal earns something back — a lower rate, a perk, priority treatment. Medicare plans don't work that way.

There's no discount for having been enrolled five years instead of one. Staying and switching are treated exactly the same by the plan itself.

Why this pattern is easy to miss

It's not a lack of attention — it's that nothing forces the comparison. The renewal happens automatically, the mail pile gets set aside, and there's rarely an obvious reason to stop and check.

That's true for almost everyone, not just people who don't like paperwork. The system is built so that not checking is the path of least resistance.

How the cost shows up

Nobody sends a bill labeled "cost of not comparing." It shows up quietly, in small shifts that add up:

  • A premium that creeps upward while a comparable plan nearby holds steady or offers $0.
  • A prescription that moves to a higher tier on your plan's formulary, while another plan's formulary didn't move it.
  • A network that narrows, dropping a specialist you use, while a different plan added that same specialist.
  • Extra benefits that shrink — like a dental or over-the-counter allowance — while a competing plan's grew.

None of these show up unless you actually look. That's the whole mechanism.

A simple way to picture it

Imagine two neighbors who started on identical plans the same year. One compares every fall; the other lets the plan auto-renew without a second look, year after year.

Nothing dramatic has to happen for a gap to open between them. A tier change here, a network change there — small shifts that only one of them ever noticed, because only one of them was checking.

Why comparing doesn't cost anything

A plan comparison itself is free. It doesn't obligate you to switch, sign anything, or make a decision on the spot.

The only real cost is the ten minutes it takes, and that's the entire trade being weighed here — ten minutes against a year of not knowing.

The ten minutes it actually takes

A plan review is short by design: your current plan, your doctors, your medications, and what else is available in your area for the coming year. We walk through exactly what that call looks like, minute by minute, in an upcoming post.

What comparing does not mean

Comparing is not the same as switching. Plenty of people compare every year and choose to stay exactly where they are.

The difference isn't the outcome — it's that staying becomes a decision you made, instead of a default you never looked at.

Think of it less like shopping around and more like an annual checkup for your coverage. You don't switch doctors every year either — you just make sure the one you have is still the right fit.

The bottom line

There's nothing wrong with staying on a plan you like. The only thing worth avoiding is staying on a plan you've never actually checked.

Does comparing plans cost anything?

No. A plan comparison and review is free, and there's no obligation to change anything as a result of it.

If I compare, am I obligated to switch?

Not at all. Comparing simply gives you the information to decide. Many people compare and choose to stay exactly where they are — the difference is that it's now an informed choice.

How often should I compare plans?

Once a year, generally during Open Enrollment (October 15 to December 7), is enough for most people, since that's also when plans finalize what's changing for the next year.

What if my current plan really is still the best fit?

Then comparing confirms that, and you can stay with confidence instead of by default. Either outcome is a good use of ten minutes.
