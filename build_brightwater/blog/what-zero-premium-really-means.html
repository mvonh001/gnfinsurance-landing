Zero-Premium Plans: What 'Free' Really Means

A $0 monthly premium is a real number, not a marketing trick. It's also just one line on a longer bill.

September 22·4 min read
By Michael von Heesen — Licensed Insurance Agent, Brightwater Insurance Group

Plenty of Medicare Advantage plans advertise a $0 monthly premium. It's a real number, and it naturally raises a question: what does "free" actually include, and what's it leaving out?

What $0 premium actually means

It means there's no separate monthly bill for the plan itself, on top of what you already pay Medicare. That's the entire scope of the claim.

The standard Part B premium is still owed to Medicare directly. A $0 plan premium doesn't change that — it's a separate line, paid separately.

What "zero" doesn't include

A $0 premium describes one line on the bill, not the whole bill. Depending on the plan and the care you use, you can still owe:

  • Deductibles for hospital stays or prescription drugs.
  • Copays for doctor visits, specialist visits, or emergency care.
  • Coinsurance — a percentage of the cost, rather than a flat fee.
  • Out-of-pocket costs that build up over the year, up to the plan's maximum.

Why plans can offer $0 at all

Medicare pays Medicare Advantage plans directly for each member enrolled. That funding is part of why a plan can set its own premium to $0 and still cover its costs.

In plain terms: $0 premium isn't a discount or a loss leader. It's simply how some plans choose to price the monthly bill.

How to find your own answer

The only way to know what a $0 plan would actually cost you is to check it against your own situation, not a general description. A few concrete steps help:

  • Look up your medications on the plan's drug list, or formulary, and note the tier each one falls into.
  • Confirm your doctors and pharmacy are listed as in-network for that specific plan.
  • Ask what the deductible and out-of-pocket maximum are, since those numbers vary plan to plan even at $0 premium.

When a $0 premium plan is genuinely the right fit

For a lot of people, it is. If your doctors and pharmacy are in-network, your regular medications sit at a low pricing tier, and you're generally healthy, a $0 premium plan can be excellent value.

Honest framing matters here — this isn't always the "cheap version." Sometimes it's simply the right plan.

When it's worth a second look

If you see several specialists, take an expensive medication, or travel out of state often, the deductibles and network rules matter more than the premium line. A $0 plan can still work — it just needs a closer check first.

The honest bottom line

$0 premium isn't a red flag, and it isn't a golden ticket either. It's a starting price, and starting prices are only useful once you check what they include for you specifically.

Is a $0 premium Medicare Advantage plan really free?

It means no separate monthly bill for the plan itself. You can still owe deductibles, copays, and coinsurance depending on the care you use, so "free" refers to the premium line specifically, not total cost.

Do I still pay my Part B premium if I choose a $0 plan?

Generally yes. The standard Part B premium is paid to Medicare directly and is separate from whatever a Medicare Advantage plan charges on top of it, even when that plan charges nothing.

Are zero-premium plans lower quality?

Not inherently. A $0 premium reflects how the plan is funded, not the quality of its network or benefits. Each plan needs to be checked on its own terms.

How do I know if a $0 premium plan is a good fit for me?

Check whether your doctors and pharmacy are in-network, whether your prescriptions are covered at a low tier, and what the deductibles and copays look like for the care you actually use.
