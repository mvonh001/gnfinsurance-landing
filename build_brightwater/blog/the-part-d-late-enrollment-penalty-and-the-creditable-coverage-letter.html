The Part D Late Enrollment Penalty and the “Creditable Coverage” Letter You Shouldn't Throw Away

A one-page notice that arrives every fall can quietly save you from a charge that lasts for life. Here's what it is and why it matters.

October 12·5 min read
By Michael von Heesen — Licensed Insurance Agent, Brightwater Insurance Group

Every fall, many people who still get drug coverage through a job, a union, or a retiree plan receive a short notice that looks like routine paperwork. It usually says something like “Important Notice About Your Prescription Drug Coverage and Medicare.” It's easy to toss. It's worth keeping — because that one page can be the proof that keeps you clear of the Part D late enrollment penalty.

What the Part D penalty is

Medicare expects people to have drug coverage once they're eligible. If you go more than 63 days in a row without Part D or other creditable drug coverage after your Initial Enrollment Period ends, and you later join a Medicare drug plan, a penalty is usually added to your monthly premium.

Two things make it different from most fees:

  • It's permanent. In general, you pay it for as long as you have Medicare drug coverage.
  • It grows with the gap. The longer you went without creditable coverage, the larger it is.

How it's figured, in percentages

The penalty is 1% of the national base beneficiary premium for each full month you went without creditable drug coverage. Medicare sets that base premium every year, so the penalty can shift a little from year to year, but the percentage mechanic stays the same.

A simple example: someone who went 20 full months without creditable coverage would generally have 20% of the base premium added to their Part D premium each month. Thirty months would mean 30%. The math is simple; the point is that it keeps going.

What “creditable coverage” means

Creditable drug coverage is coverage that's expected to pay, on average, at least as much as Medicare's standard drug benefit. Coverage from an employer, a union, TRICARE, VA health care, or a retiree plan may qualify — but not automatically. Each plan has to be checked, and the answer can change from one year to the next as both the plan and Medicare's standard benefit change.

If your drug coverage is creditable, you can delay Part D without a penalty. When that coverage ends, you generally get a Special Enrollment Period to join a Medicare drug plan — and as long as there isn't a gap of more than 63 days, no penalty applies.

Why that letter matters so much

Employers and unions that offer drug coverage are required to tell their Medicare-eligible members each year whether that coverage is creditable. That's the notice that tends to show up in early fall, before the Annual Enrollment Period begins on October 15.

Here's why to keep every one of them:

  • It's your proof. When you eventually join a Part D plan, you may be asked whether you had creditable coverage and when. The letters answer that question in writing.
  • It tells you if something changed. If a notice ever says your coverage is not creditable, that's your signal to look at Medicare drug coverage before a gap starts adding up.
  • Memories fade; paper doesn't. Years later, it can be hard to reconstruct exactly which months were covered. A folder of yearly notices settles it.

A simple habit: keep a folder labeled “Medicare – drug coverage” and drop each year's notice in it.

Who usually doesn't pay the penalty

  • People who had creditable drug coverage with no gap longer than 63 days.
  • People who qualify for Extra Help, Medicare's program that helps with drug costs for people with limited income and resources.
  • People who can show they were given inaccurate information that their coverage was creditable may be able to have a penalty reconsidered.

If you're told you owe it

If a drug plan notifies you of a late enrollment penalty and you believe you had creditable coverage, you can ask for a reconsideration. The notice explains the steps and the time limit to respond. This is exactly where those saved letters do their job.

The bottom line

The Part D penalty is avoidable for most people, and the tool that avoids it is surprisingly ordinary: a yearly letter and a folder to keep it in. If you're still working, still on a retiree plan, or helping a parent who is, check this fall's notice and file it away.

Common questions

Is the Part D late enrollment penalty a one-time charge?

No. It is generally added to your monthly Part D premium for as long as you have Medicare drug coverage. The amount can shift slightly each year because it is tied to a national base premium that Medicare updates annually.

What if I lost my creditable coverage letter?

Ask your employer's benefits or HR office, your union, or the plan itself for a copy. They can usually reissue it or confirm in writing whether your drug coverage was creditable and for which dates.

Can I challenge a penalty I think is wrong?

Yes. If your drug plan tells you that you owe a penalty, you can ask for a reconsideration. The notice explains how, and there is a time limit to respond, so read it promptly and gather proof of your prior coverage.

Does everyone who enrolls late pay the penalty?

Not everyone. People who qualify for Extra Help with drug costs generally do not pay it, and a gap of 63 days or less without creditable coverage does not trigger it.
