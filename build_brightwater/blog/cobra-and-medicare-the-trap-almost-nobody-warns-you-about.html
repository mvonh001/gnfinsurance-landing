COBRA and Medicare: The Trap Almost Nobody Warns You About

Keeping your old job's health plan can feel like the safe choice after 65. Here's why the Medicare clock may already be running.

November 9·5 min read
By Michael von Heesen — Licensed Insurance Agent, Brightwater Insurance Group

When a job ends after 65 — a retirement, a layoff, a buyout — the benefits paperwork usually includes an offer to keep your same health plan through COBRA. It feels like the safe choice: same card, same doctors, nothing new to learn. For someone who is eligible for Medicare, though, COBRA can quietly set up one of the most common and costly enrollment mistakes there is.

What COBRA is (and isn't)

COBRA is a federal law that lets you continue your former employer's group health coverage for a set period after you leave — often up to 18 months. You generally pay the full cost of the coverage yourself, plus a small administrative charge.

The key point for Medicare: COBRA is not coverage based on current employment. It's the same plan, but Medicare treats it very differently from the coverage you had while you were actively working.

The trap: the clock starts when the job ends

If you delayed Part B because you had coverage through your own (or your spouse's) current job at a large employer, you generally get a Special Enrollment Period to sign up for Part B without a penalty. That window is 8 months, and it begins the month after the employment or the employer coverage ends — whichever happens first.

Here's where people get caught. For most people, the employment ends first. So the 8-month clock starts the month after your last day of work — not when your COBRA runs out. Someone who stays on COBRA for 18 months and then calls to sign up for Part B may find the Special Enrollment Period closed nearly a year earlier.

What happens if you miss it

  • You may have to wait. Without a Special Enrollment Period, you'd generally sign up during the General Enrollment Period, January 1 through March 31, with coverage starting the month after you enroll. That can leave a gap.
  • A lasting penalty may apply. The Part B late enrollment penalty is 10% of the standard Part B premium for each full 12-month period you could have had Part B but didn't — and it generally lasts as long as you have Part B. Months on COBRA count toward that gap.
  • COBRA may not pay the way you expect. Once you're eligible for Medicare, Medicare is generally the primary payer and COBRA is secondary. If you never enrolled in Part B, a COBRA plan may pay only what it would have paid after Medicare — leaving you with bills Medicare would have covered.

Order matters

The sequence of events changes the rules:

  • Already on Medicare, then offered COBRA: you can generally take COBRA as well. It typically pays after Medicare and may help with things Medicare doesn't cover, such as some dental or vision benefits.
  • On COBRA first, then become eligible for Medicare: your COBRA coverage may end once you become entitled to Medicare. Check your plan's notice carefully.

Part D works a little differently

For drug coverage, the question is whether your COBRA drug coverage is creditable — expected to pay at least as much as Medicare's standard drug benefit. If it is, it can keep you clear of the Part D late enrollment penalty while it lasts. When it ends, you generally have 63 days to join a Medicare drug plan without a gap. Keep the plan's creditable coverage notice as proof.

Where COBRA still makes sense

COBRA isn't always the wrong move. It can be a sensible bridge for a spouse or dependent who isn't yet eligible for Medicare, keeping them covered while the person who turned 65 moves to Medicare. It can also fill in around Medicare for a while if you're already enrolled. The mistake is using COBRA instead of Medicare, not alongside it.

A simple rule of thumb

If you're 65 or older and your job is ending, treat your last day of work as the date that matters for Part B — and make your Medicare decision within those 8 months, whatever you decide about COBRA. Write the date down. If you're unsure where you stand, Medicare, Social Security, or Florida's SHINE program can confirm your options, and a licensed agent can walk through the timing with you.

Common questions

Does COBRA count as employer coverage for delaying Part B?

Generally, no. COBRA is not coverage based on current employment, so it does not extend your Part B Special Enrollment Period. The 8-month window usually starts the month after your job ends, even if COBRA continues.

Can I have COBRA and Medicare at the same time?

Often, yes — especially if you were already on Medicare before COBRA started. In that case Medicare usually pays first and COBRA pays second. If COBRA came first, your plan may end it once you become entitled to Medicare.

Does COBRA drug coverage protect me from the Part D penalty?

It can, if the plan's drug coverage is creditable. Keep the plan's notice that says so, and join a Medicare drug plan within 63 days after the COBRA coverage ends.

Should my spouse keep COBRA if I move to Medicare?

It may make sense if your spouse is under 65 and not yet eligible for Medicare. Medicare is individual, so each person's situation and timing should be looked at separately.
